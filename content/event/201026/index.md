Gustaf Winstrand (Foreign Policy Advisor, Swedish embassy), who was announced on the poster, was unable to attend.

## Summary

**From the Cold War to 2022.** The Arctic became strategically important during the Cold War as a bastion for nuclear submarines and second-strike capability. After Gorbachev's 1987 Murmansk speech called for a "zone of peace and cooperation", cooperation began with science diplomacy (the International Arctic Science Committee, 1990), followed by Norwegian-initiated military-environmental cleanup of nuclear and other waste on the Kola Peninsula and the founding of the Arctic Council in 1996. Western states kept treating the Arctic as an exceptional zone of cooperation even after Russia's wars in Chechnya and Georgia and the annexation of Crimea. That ended with the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, during Russia's Arctic Council chairmanship. Working-group activity has since resumed digitally under Norway's chairmanship.

**Japan, South Korea and Russia.** Asian states' interest in the Arctic grew after Russia's 2007 flag-planting at the North Pole, and Japan, South Korea and China became Arctic Council observers in 2013. Their engagement with Russia has been mainly economic: Japanese stakes in Yamal LNG and Arctic LNG 2, and South Korean-built LNG carriers. After 2022 this came under strain, but the Hormuz crisis pushed Japan and Korea back towards Russian energy, and Korea under President Lee Jae-myung has shown new interest in the Northern Sea Route while rarely mentioning the talks with Russia this involves.

**Why Trump wants Greenland.** Greenland (Kalaallit Nunaat) is a nation with a right to self-determination, not just a resource-rich island. Trump's interest dates to 2019 and grew out of Washington's concern about Chinese investment attempts in Greenlandic mines and airports, which Copenhagen blocked. Lamazhapov argued there is no compelling strategic reason for a new arrangement: the 1951 defence agreement already gives the US wide access, including Pituffik Space Base, and Trump himself has said rare earths are not the reason. Sphere-of-influence thinking, a "strategic myth" serving domestic politics, and the appeal of a presidential legacy are more plausible explanations. The new US–Denmark–Greenland agreement is not simply a repeat of 1951: it has no expiry date and commits Greenland to seek NATO membership even if it becomes independent, with trade-offs that Danish public debate has tended to downplay.

**Critical minerals.** "Critical minerals" is largely a US term; the EU speaks of critical raw materials. Rare earths are 17 different elements, and China dominates both mining and downstream uses such as permanent magnets. Greenland has notable deposits, among them Kvanefjeld (stalled by Greenland's 2021 uranium-mining ban) and the US-controlled Tanbreez project. Because rare earth mining is rarely profitable, new mines in Greenland or the European Arctic would need heavy subsidies and a rebuilt supply chain to compete with Chinese producers. Russia, by contrast, has little direct interest in Greenland beyond the GIUK gap and disinformation.

**The Nordics and the EU.** As small or middle powers, the Nordic countries could once turn economic capital into political influence under the rules-based order. With great power politics returning, they are seeking closer bilateral ties with larger partners, such as Norway with the United Kingdom. The EU's new Arctic strategy, released on the day of the panel, marks a striking shift from climate and environmental protection towards security and critical raw materials.

**Audience questions.**

- **Hybrid threats:** Intent is hard to establish when analysts depend on open sources, and states tend to overstate each other's hostility. Still, real threats to infrastructure exist, digitalisation has created new vulnerabilities, and NATO's longer border with Russia after Finland and Sweden joined has changed the picture. Even the threat of sabotage imposes real costs on defenders.
- **NATO enlargement:** The accession of Finland and Sweden strengthens European defence and is a strategic setback for Russia, whose war has brought NATO closer to Moscow and St Petersburg.
- **What a secure Arctic looks like:** The debate has moved from environmental and human security to hard security. There is no common European vision, and NATO is the only organisation that thinks about it holistically. In North America, the US focus on "hemispheric defence" unsettles Canada and Denmark, while Chinese and Russian activity in the Bering Sea is mostly symbolic.
- **Climate change and the Northern Sea Route:** Russia now accepts that climate change is real and highlights its expected benefits. But the Northern Sea Route lacks the intermediate ports that container shipping relies on, and seasonal ice still requires icebreaker support. Russia's target of 80 million tonnes by 2024 ended at about 37 million, and the 2030 target of 130 million tonnes is unlikely to be met. Arctic shipping is likely to stay a small stream in global trade.

Asked which topics students should research, Lamazhapov pointed to Indigenous issues (approached respectfully), the EU in the Arctic, and the open question of how to keep climate change central without framing it only as a security issue.
